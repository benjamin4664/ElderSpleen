Ancient Poems

A Thousand Years, a Thousand Emotions, an Eternal Beauty

From Sappho to Hypatia, a thousand years of voices. A thousand emotions: farewell, longing, jealousy, tenderness, the fear of being forgotten. And the same beauty, intact, reaching us today with all its force.

Nearly twenty-six centuries ago, on an island in the Aegean Sea, a woman sang of parting, of crowns of violets, and of the turmoil of watching the beloved sit across from her. A millennium later, in Cyrene, the philosopher Synesius wrote to Hypatia, his heart heavy with war, to tell her that if he ever left his homeland, it would only be to go to her.

Between these two voices, nothing has aged: not the jealousy, not the tenderness, not the fear that the other will forget us, not the desire to savor the present before it fades. Tibullus dreams of a simple life beside Delia. Catullus counts his kisses. Propertius fears that his lover’s devotion will not outlive his funeral, and that his ashes will be forgotten. Gregory of Nazianzus, a fourth-century poet and bishop, puts into verse the loss and weariness of a soul seeking peace. And other texts declare, with the same fervor, that true love is not a fire that dies with the years. They are not distant figures. They are voices that still tremble.

This selection does not step outside philosophy: it is its sensitive continuation. It springs from Lucretius, who wished to free humankind from the fear of death, and whose most heartbreaking passage recounts the sacrifice of Iphigenia, a young girl led to the altar by superstition. Tantum religio potuit suadere malorum, he writes: so much evil could religion inspire. That grief, too, reaches us undiminished. It springs from Epicurus, who taught how to enjoy the present without disturbance. What are we to do with the brevity of our lives? Elegy answers differently. It does not demonstrate; it murmurs. Tibullus’s carpe diem, Catullus’s “let us live, my Lesbia”, Propertius’s dread: these are the questions of the Garden, asked by trembling voices.

Each piece keeps the text as its translators have handed it down, without cuts or rewording. The music, electronic and spare, makes no attempt to modernize them. It leaves them room, so that the breath of Sappho, Tibullus, Catullus, Propertius, Gregory of Nazianzus, Synesius, and that of Lucretius who precedes them, may regain, for a few minutes, its full presence.

This list is still being built, and will remain so for a long time. It will never reflect more than a tiny part of the richness of this heritage: thousands of verses, from Greece, Rome and beyond, still wait to be reread and heard. This selection is an invitation, not an inventory. From these few voices, each listener can set out to meet the others.

Empires pass, languages change, manuscripts are lost. Words endure. A thousand emotions, an eternal beauty: this is what these pieces hope to let you hear.
